--- DRAFT ---

My work comes from two places that shape who I am as an artist: disability and life outside the centre of Toronto. Both are OAC priority groups.

I live with invisible disabilities and am neurodivergent. Disability is also a theme in my art. A Song for Hephaestus explored disability, injustice and the liberatory potential of creative labour. Living with disability has taught me how often spaces are built for some people and not others, and it is why access sits at the centre of this project: we will work with partners to learn who wants to take part, and to make our spaces and sessions work for them.

I grew up in poverty in rural communities and now work outside downtown Toronto, in a rural school community near Tottenham and a retirement community in Thornhill. Growing up rural taught me how much creative life depends on the people around you, and how rarely communities like these are the focus of arts funding. I want to create art with people where they already live and learn, not ask them to travel to it.

My artistic influences are mythology and folklore, folk music and theatre. I use well-known stories and rich songwriting to give people an easy way in, then make room for new voices. As a queer person, I am also drawn to stories about difference and belonging, and about finding community in unexpected places.

The communities I work with include children who live and learn in a rural, nature-based school, and older adults who have lived all over the world. Working with both, I have seen how much each group gains from the other, and that is the work I want to keep doing.
